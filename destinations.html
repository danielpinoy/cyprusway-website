    Destinations across Cyprus

    Six distinctive regions, each with its own landscape, history, and character

    Paphos

    Paphos sits on Cyprus's western coast, a UNESCO World Heritage city wrapped around a scenic harbour. Its archaeological park holds some of the finest Roman floor mosaics in the Eastern Mediterranean, and the Tombs of the Kings — underground burial chambers carved from solid rock — stretch across a coastal plateau just north of the harbour. The old town has kept its traditional character with narrow streets, family-run tavernas, and a slower pace that contrasts with the resort strip along Poseidonos Avenue.

    Beyond the city, the Akamas Peninsula stretches north — a wild finger of coast with the Blue Lagoon at its tip, the Avakas Gorge cutting inland, and the Baths of Aphrodite tucked into the hillside. Coral Bay, 12 kilometres north of the centre, is one of the district's most popular sandy beaches. Inland, traditional villages like Tsada and Tala offer wine-country dining and mountain views. The Aphrodite Cultural Route connects sites tied to the goddess across the district, ending at Petra tou Romiou — Aphrodite's Rock — where the sea breaks against a stack of limestone rising from the shoreline.

    Limassol

    Limassol is Cyprus's cosmopolitan waterfront city, stretching along a broad bay on the south coast. The modern marina and the adjacent Molos promenade form the city's social centre — a landscaped seafront walk lined with cafes, sculpture gardens, and views across the Mediterranean. The old town, just inland, is a dense grid of narrow streets with a growing food and bar scene, anchored by a medieval castle where Richard the Lionheart is said to have married.

    To the west, the Kourion archaeological site sits on a cliff overlooking the sea — a Greco-Roman city with a restored amphitheatre that still hosts summer concerts. Further west, Kolossi Castle marks the edge of the wine region. The Krassochoria villages — Omodos, Koilani, Lofou — climb into the Troodos foothills, producing some of Cyprus's best wines, including the island's signature sweet Commandaria. Limassol is also known for its festivals: the Carnival in February or March fills the streets, and the Wine Festival in September turns the Municipal Gardens into an open-air celebration.

    Larnaca

    Larnaca stretches along a sheltered bay on Cyprus's southeast coast, its palm-lined Finikoudes Promenade forming the city's defining feature. The Church of St. Lazarus, a 9th-century Byzantine stone church in the town centre, is one of the island's most significant religious sites. Just west of the city, the Larnaca Salt Lake fills with water in winter and, from November to March, attracts thousands of flamingos. On its shore, Hala Sultan Tekke — an Ottoman mosque and shrine — is one of the most important Islamic pilgrimage sites in the Mediterranean.

    Offshore, the Zenobia wreck — a Swedish roll-on/roll-off ferry that sank in 1980 — is consistently ranked among the world's top ten wreck dives. The region also holds some of Cyprus's oldest human history: the Choirokoitia Neolithic settlement, a UNESCO site, dates back nearly 9,000 years. Inland, the hill village of Lefkara is known across Europe for its traditional lace and silver craft, a trade that goes back centuries.

    Ayia Napa

    Ayia Napa, on Cyprus's far eastern coast, is known for two very different identities that coexist within the same coastline. The town centre is one of the Mediterranean's most energetic nightlife districts, with a dense cluster of clubs, bars, and music venues around the central square. By day — and a short distance away — the beaches tell a different story entirely. Nissi Beach, a long arc of white sand with shallow turquoise water, is among the most photographed beaches on the island. Makronissos Beach, just to the southwest, offers a quieter stretch of sand backed by low cliffs.

    Cape Greco National Park, at the easternmost tip, is a limestone headland of sea caves, coastal walking trails, and dramatic cliff-top views. The sea caves are accessible by boat or by scrambling down from the cliffs. The Love Bridge — a natural rock arch — is one of the most photographed spots on the island. Offshore, the cape offers some of Cyprus's best diving and snorkelling. The area also has water parks, go-karting, and boat trips to the nearby Blue Lagoon.

    Protaras

    Protaras sits just north of Ayia Napa on the eastern coast, but offers a calmer, more family-oriented atmosphere. Fig Tree Bay — named for a lone fig tree on the beach — is the area's best-known stretch of sand: shallow, clear water, a Blue Flag designation, and a gently shelving shore that makes it particularly suitable for children. The bay is backed by a coastal path that runs south toward Cape Greco and north toward Paralimni.

    Konnos Bay, tucked between Protaras and Cape Greco, is a smaller, more sheltered cove with green hillside rising behind it and calm water that's good for swimming and snorkelling. The area has several water parks and a popular evening water-and-light show on the seafront. Protaras appeals to families and couples who want proximity to the attractions of the eastern coast without the late-night energy of Ayia Napa.

    Troodos Mountains

    The Troodos range rises across the centre of Cyprus, its highest peak — Mount Olympus — reaching 1,952 metres and holding snow through winter. The mountains feel like a different country from the coast: pine forest replaces scrubland, stone-built villages replace resort strips, and the air is noticeably cooler even in summer. Ten painted Byzantine churches scattered through the villages, with their distinctive steep roofs and vivid interior frescoes, are collectively a UNESCO World Heritage site.

    The Kykkos Monastery, the richest and most famous of Cyprus's monasteries, sits in the Marathasa Valley with a museum of religious art and a mosaic-covered courtyard. The village of Omodos anchors the wine region with its cobbled square, the Holy Cross monastery, and wineries producing zivania and Commandaria. Platres, a resort village with a cooler climate, is the starting point for walks to the Caledonia and Millomeri waterfalls. Kalopanagiotis, in the Marathasa Valley, combines traditional stone architecture with sulphur springs and a spa. The Artemis Trail circles the summit and the Atalante Trail runs below it — both offering long views across the island.

    Curated places across every category

    Beaches

    From the famous arcs of Nissi Beach and Fig Tree Bay to quiet coves like Konnos Bay and remote shores like Lara Beach.

    Archaeological Sites

    Kourion, Paphos mosaics, Tombs of the Kings, Choirokoitia — Cyprus has 9,000 years of layered history to explore.

    Kykkos Monastery, painted Troodos churches, Agios Neophytos, and St. Lazarus — faith and architecture across the island.

    Omodos, Lefkara, the Krassochoria wine villages — traditional Cyprus with village squares, wineries, and local crafts.

    Castles & Fortifications

    Limassol Castle, Kolossi Castle, Paphos Castle, the Venetian Walls of Nicosia — centuries of strategic architecture.
